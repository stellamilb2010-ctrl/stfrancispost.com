Death and the Pharaoh: How the Media Created a Curse

By Bernardo Nery · October 7, 2026

104 years after the discovery of Tutankhamun’s tomb, the legend of the “pharaoh’s curse” continues to fascinate the world. But how much of it was actually true?

The discovery of the tomb

On the 2nd of November 1922 Dr. Howard Carter received the news that would change the history of archeology forever. The tomb of the third-to-last Pharaoh of the 18th Egyptian dynasty was kept a secret for more than three millennia. The expedition was set for the Valley of Kings on the west bank of the Nile. At the time the Valley was considered by archeological scholars as completely empty, without any great king's tomb left, and indeed king Tutankhamun wasn't a great king like his father king Akhennaton, building a new capital or changing the empire's religion. Tutankhamun ruled for roughly a decade, and unlike some of his predecessors, he left behind no major military conquests or revolutionary building projects. You might be asking yourself, “So, why was this guy so famous? ” His fame wasn't conquered from his actions in life but by his legacy in death.

The Egyptian empire was famous since its foundation. And the wealth of its emperors was pillaged for many centuries making it harder for archeologists to understand the complexity of the northern African civilization. The relatively obscure nature of Tutankhamun's reign made the discovery even more remarkable: the world had suddenly found an almost perfectly preserved royal tomb belonging to a pharaoh whose historical importance had previously been limited.

The curse and the media's role

A few months after the discovery of the tomb on April 5th, 1923, Lord Carnarvon, the wealthy British aristocrat who had financed Howard Carter’s excavation died in Cairo. His death followed an infected mosquito bite that he had cut while shaving that led to erysipelas and septicemia. For journalists, however, the timing was perfect. An ancient tomb has just been opened and one of the men responsible for the disturbance of the pharaoh's eternal rest was suddenly dead.

Carnarvon’s death was only the beginning of the story. As other people connected to Tutankhamun’s tomb and to Dr. Howard Carter died in the years that followed, their deaths were increasingly presented as part of the same mysterious pattern. To the public, the growing list of deaths seemed to suggest that Pharaoh’s curse was real.

Carnarvon’s death could have been reported simply as the death of a wealthy British aristocrat following a serious infection. Instead, its connection to Tutankhamun gave journalists a much more dramatic narrative. The timing made the two events appear connected, even though it was not proof of a supernatural cause.

As more deaths were associated with people connected to the tomb, newspapers could present them as additional pieces of the same story. The deaths supported the idea of a curse and became part of the narrative, while the many people who survived their involvement with the excavation attracted far less attention.

The fungus and the truth

Researchers have found fungi such as Aspergillus niger and Aspergillus flavus in some ancient mummies. Certain species of Aspergillus can cause respiratory problems, particularly in people with weakened immune systems. This might seem like an explanation for the deaths associated with Tutankhamun’s tomb. However, there is an important problem: there is no evidence that a fungus from Tutankhamun’s tomb caused Lord Carnarvon’s death. His illness developed after an infected wound, and experts have questioned whether exposure to microorganisms inside the tomb could explain an illness occurring months after the initial exposure.

In fact, the scientific evidence makes the story considerably less mysterious. A study of people associated with the excavation found no significant relationship between entering the tomb and dying at a younger age. Carter himself, one of the people most closely associated with the discovery, lived until 1939, almost two decades after the tomb was opened. The fungus theory, therefore, does not replace the curse with another definite explanation.

More than a century after Tutankhamun’s tomb was opened, there is still no scientific evidence that an ancient supernatural curse killed those who entered it. Scientific explanations, including the possibility of microorganisms inside ancient tombs, have been investigated, but none has established a biological cause for the supposed curse.
