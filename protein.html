THE PROTEIN PARADOX: When does “HEALTHY” become UNHEALTHY?

By Gabriella Termini · October 27, 2026

Protein is absolutely everywhere. Go walk into a supermarket and you'll find it difficult to miss. It is in cereals, yogurt, bread, pasta, snack bars, drinks, ice cream and even chips. On social media, influencers share their “high-protein” breakfasts, lunches, snacks, and dinners. Fitness creators count their daily grams while promoting products that are advertised with the promise of more protein. But protein is not just another food trend. It is a crucial part of the human body and its essential functions. It helps build and repair tissues, supports muscles and bones, and plays a role in enzymes, hormones, immunity, skin and hair. Therefore there's no doubt why protein has such a strong reputation for being healthy. But this creates a complication: When does trying to be healthy become unhealthy? The problem is not protein itself. The problem begins when “getting enough” is no longer just “getting enough”.

Protein: An Essential Macro Nutrient

Protein is made up of amino acids. There are 20 amino acids, nine of which are absolutely essential and can only come from food. When we eat protein, digestion breaks it down into amino acids, which are absorbed into the bloodstream and transported to cells all around the body.

These amino acids are used throughout the body. They help build and repair tissues and are involved in muscles, bones, skin, hair, enzymes, hormones, antibodies and other processes. Protein can also be used as an energy source. In other words, protein deserves a beneficial reputation as a very important nutrient. But being important does not mean that more is always better.

That distinction between them is becoming increasingly difficult to see. Especially in a culture where protein has developed to become the one source described as a “health halo.” The word has become associated with strength, fitness, weight management, attractiveness and healthy living. As a result, simply seeing “high protein” on a packet can make a product appear healthier, even before considering what else is actually in it.

From Getting Enough to Getting Too Much

Protein intake is extremely individual. It depends on factors including age, exercise, health and dietary patterns. One general recommendation gives the adult daily requirement as 0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day, while another set of guidelines recommends between 1.2 and 1.6 grams per kilogram. At the same time, many healthy adults in the United States and other developed countries are unlikely to have a protein deficiency. Most people already consume enough protein. Yet, the modern protein trend often focuses on something different: maximising protein.

Some fad diets promote up to 200 to 400 grams of protein a day. Social media posts encourage people to reach specific daily targets, usually the highest protein possible with the addition of protein powders, shakes and bars that make it easier to keep adding more protein to the diet. This creates an important difference between eating enough protein and constantly trying to increase protein intake. The idea sounds simple: if protein is good for you, surely more must be better, but nutrition is rarely that simple, and each body is extremely complex and unique.

How Protein Became “The Super Nutrient”

Protein's current popularity did not appear overnight. In the 1890s, the USDA recommended more than 110 grams of protein a day for working men, influenced by the idea that protein was a source of muscular energy, needed to optimize manual labour. In the 1950s and 1960s, the “World Protein Gap” occurred where protein deficiencies in children became a major nutritional concern, threatening children's health in some parts of the world. Protein powders appeared on the market in the 1950s mostly used by body builders and athletes who needed an efficient way to boost their protein intake. Later, low-carbohydrate and low-fat diets helped push protein even further into the spotlight.

As fitness moved beyond professional athletes and bodybuilders, and became part of the mainstream culture, protein became increasingly associated with strength and physical health. During the pandemic, interest in health and wellness increased, and protein became associated with health, strength and immunity. Then came social media. Searches for “high protein diet” reached an all-time high in January of 2025. Protein has moved from being a specific nutritional concern to an everyday lifestyle trend.

When Everything Becomes “High Protein”

The change can be seen most clearly in the supermarket. Protein has been added to foods that traditionally were not particularly associated with it; cereal, bread, wraps, pancakes, cookies, chips, ice cream, desserts, drinks, snack bars, pasta, etc. Some sources estimate that the number of high-protein products sold worldwide has quadrupled over the past decade. Other sources report that the number of high-protein shakes and drinks increased by 122% between 2020 and 2024. The result is a food environment where protein is no longer simply something naturally found in meals. It has become something that can and must be added to almost anything. And there is a reason companies are doing it: Protein has become big business.

The Business of Being “Healthy”

The protein industry is now worth billions. One source puts the protein industry at $114 billion currently, while projecting the protein ingredients market to grow from $93.2 billion in 2025 to $182.5 billion by 2035. A protein startup network has also been projected to generate $20 billion in value between 2025 and 2035. Protein bars, powders, shakes, drinks, cereals and snacks are all part of this expanding market. For consumers, the appeal is easy to understand: protein is associated with health, fitness, convenience, energy and weight management. For companies, that association creates an opportunity. The more strongly consumers connect protein with health, the more valuable the word “protein” becomes on a package.

This does not mean that every high-protein product is unhealthy or that companies are deliberately misleading consumers. It does, however, mean that the word “protein” can influence how we perceive a food, and this perception can sometimes be deceiving. A product can be high in protein and still contain significant amounts of other ingredients such as added sugar, sodium or saturated fat. “High protein” gives us a small piece of information. It does not tell us everything.

The Protein Influencer

The supermarket is only part of the story. On social media, protein has become almost a language of its own. An average post might promise to show you how to reach 150 grams of protein in a day. Another might tell you exactly how much protein you “need”. Others show high-protein breakfasts, protein-packed snacks or replacements for foods considered too low in protein. Fitness influencers frequently include protein quantities in “What I Eat in a Day” videos, while high-protein recipes have become common on platforms such as TikTok. The influence is significant.

One Cargill report found that 25% of consumers considered websites and social media platforms key influences on their eating habits. Among Gen Z respondents, 61% said they value influencer advice when making dietary decisions. The result is that protein is no longer just something people learn about from nutrition information and professionals. It is something they constantly see everywhere. And when the same message appears again and again, it begins to feel less like a suggestion and more like a rule.

When Protein Becomes a Number

Possibly one of the biggest changes is that protein is incredibly easy to measure. Grams. Servings. Scoops. Shakes. Daily targets. There is nothing inherently wrong with knowing how much protein a food contains. The problem begins when the number becomes an obsession, and more important than the food itself. The information provided identifies possible consequences of excessive protein tracking, including food anxiety, guilt, restrictive eating, fear of foods considered “low protein”, obsessive tracking and the feeling that missing a target means you have failed. At that point, protein is no longer simply providing nutrition. It is influencing how a person thinks about eating and goes on about everyday life.

A meal can become “good” because it contains enough protein and “bad” because it does not. A person might begin choosing foods based almost entirely on their protein content rather than considering the meal as a whole. The pursuit of health can slowly become a pursuit of a number.

When More Protein Means Less Balance

There is another problem with making one nutrient the centre of a diet: others might be “replaced”. A high-protein diet can displace carbohydrates, fats, vitamins and minerals. Focusing heavily on protein can also mean eating less fiber and fewer foods that provide other essential macronutrients and micronutrients.

One source notes that people who fill up on foods such as steaks and protein shakes may not eat enough fiber and other nutrients found mainly in plant foods. It also states that only 5% of Americans in national surveys meet the recommended intake for fiber. This creates a certain contradiction. A person can be eating more of something associated with health while decreasing their health due to lack of proper nourishment. Therefore the question is not simply: “How much protein am I getting?” It's also: “What am I missing because protein has become the priority?”

More Does Not Automatically Mean Better

Protein provides around four kilocalories (kcal) per gram, so it is also a source of energy. When the body receives more protein than it needs, excess amino acids can be broken down, and the remaining energy can contribute to stored fat, something unexpected for most. Other concerns around very high protein intake include reduced fiber intake and possible issues for people with certain existing health conditions. However, that does not mean every high-protein diet is automatically dangerous. The evidence and recommendations vary depending on the individual, which is strictly why the idea that everyone should simply “maximise protein” is too simplistic. The message is not “fear protein.” It is “more is not always better.”

The Paradox

Protein was never the problem, it is an essential nutrient: the body needs it to build and repair tissues and to carry out countless important processes. But protein has become more than a nutrient, it is now a marketing tool, a social media trend, a fitness goal and, for some people, a number that has to be reached every day. The protein boom shows how easily something genuinely healthy can become something we feel pressured to maximise. The more protein appears in products, advertisements and social media, the easier it becomes to believe that every meal should contain more. But health is not about maximising one nutrient, and the real warning sign is when the pursuit of health begins to control the person pursuing it.

Protein is healthy. Eating well is healthy. Caring about your health is healthy. But when health becomes an obsession, when food becomes a set of rigid rules, and when “enough” is never enough, it may be time to ask the fundamental question of the protein paradox: When does trying to be healthy become unhealthy?
