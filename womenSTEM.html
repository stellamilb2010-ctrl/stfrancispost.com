3 Historical Female STEM Pioneers You Should Know

By Stella Milberg · November 24, 2026

Today, women account for only 28% of the STEM workforce. Closing this stagnant gender gap can have multiple benefits, all the way from fostering diversity and inclusion, to driving economic growth. Unfortunately however, many girls struggle to find female role models in STEM, which keeps them from pursuing careers in these fields. This article will introduce you to five remarkable women who have revolutionized the world of STEM and show you that so can you.

Marie Curie

Born in 1867 in Warsaw, Marie Curie was the first woman to win the Nobel Prize and is the only woman to win it in two different science categories. Her parents were both teachers who deeply valued education, so Marie was an outstanding student from an early age. In spite of this, Marie was unable to attend the University of Warsaw, the same university where her brother studied, since women weren’t allowed to attend university under the Russian Empire. So instead she went to Paris to study physics, chemistry and mathematics.

In 1903, Marie, her husband Pierre Curie, and French Physicist Henri Becquerel won the Nobel Prize in Physics for their pioneering research on radioactivity. After Henri Becquerel’s recent discovery of radioactivity in Uranium, Marie and Pierre discovered other elements, including polonium and radium, that had the same property. Originally, Henri Becquerel and Pierre Curie would be the only ones to receive the prize, but Pierre urged that Marie share the credit as well.

Later in 1911, after her husband's passing in 1906, Marie was awarded her second Nobel Prize, this time in chemistry, for the isolation of radium. Marie is, to this day, the only person to receive the Nobel Prize in two distinct science categories.

Marie died from aplastic anemia in 1934, at the age of 66, likely due to heavy radiation exposure. Marie Curie proved to other women that they could undoubtedly succeed in STEM, making groundbreaking discoveries and receiving recognition.

Ada Lovelace

Born in 1815, English Mathematician Ada Lovelace is widely recognized as the world's first computer programmer. Ada was the daughter of Lord Bryon, a famous English poet, and Annabella Milbanke Byron, known for her mathematical aptitude, who divorced shortly after she was born. Ada’s father moved to Greece, so she was mostly raised by her mother who made sure that she received a high-quality education in mathematics, which was extremely rare for girls at that time.

In 1843, Ada translated an article by Italian mathematician and engineer Luigi Menabrea on Charles Babbage’s “Analytical Engine”, a prototype of the first computer ever. Also known as “the father of computers”, Charles Babbage — whom Ada was already friends with at the time — originally thought of his invention as merely an advanced mechanical calculator. However, Ada hypothesized that the machine could go beyond mathematical calculations to manipulate symbols, compose music, and follow a set of instructions.

When translating Luigi Menabrea’s article, Ada even added her own notes, increasing the text to three times its initial size. These notes included an algorithm designed for the Analytical Engine to compute a sequence of rational numbers which is known as the first computer program.

Ada Lovelace Day, which takes place on the second Tuesday of October every year, celebrates the achievements of women in STEM. Just like Marie Curie, Ada is an influential role model for girls whose contributions led to major breakthroughs in technology that couldn’t have been imagined without her.

Katherine Johnson

Katherine Johnson was born a child prodigy in 1918 in West Virginia. She advanced multiple years in school, starting high school at just 10 years old. At 18 years old, Katherine graduated from West Virginia State College with highest honors, earning a bachelor’s degree in both mathematics and French. She had a short-lived teaching career before being selected as one of the three Black students and first Black woman to enroll in a graduate program at West Virginia University.

In 1953, Katherine was hired by the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (NACA) to work in its West Area Computing Unit, a group of brilliant African American women who analyzed test data and performed complex mathematical calculations for aerospace engineers.

In 1960, after becoming a member of NASA's Space Task Group, Katherine coauthored a comprehensive research paper that explained the calculations needed in order to place a spacecraft into orbit, making her the first woman in her division to receive official accreditation as the author of a research report.

In 1962, Katherine played a critical role in John Glenn’s historic Friendship 7 mission, manually verifying the complex orbital mechanics equations generated by NASA's new electronic computers at Glenn's personal request before he agreed to fly. Her calculations were fundamental to the success of the Apollo moon landing program and the Space Shuttle program.

In 2015, President Barack Obama awarded Katherine the Presidential Medal of Freedom, celebrating her extraordinary contributions to mathematics, space exploration, and racial equality.
